Short answer: I looked at them, priced them, and did not do one. Nobody has ever asked.

Longer answer, which is the useful one: **I am not the person this question is usually about**, and most articles answering it fail to say so. Whether a certificate is worth it depends almost entirely on what you already have, and "no, build a portfolio instead" is very easy advice to give when you already have a portfolio.

## Why I did not

When I started moving into UX I found the same list everybody finds: institute programmes, bootcamps, platform certificates, courses with a recognisable logo at the end. Some are genuinely good. The prices ran from mildly annoying to several months of rent.

What stopped me was not scepticism about the content. It was that I already had the thing the certificate was meant to signal: work I had done, badly, that I could talk about. I came into this from building things with code, then visual work, and by the time I was calling myself a designer I had a handful of projects and a much larger handful of mistakes.

So the certificate would have bought me a signal I did not need, at a price I would have felt.

## What actually got me work

Three things, in the order they mattered.

**Projects with a decision in them.** Not screens. A decision: we could have done A or B, we did B, here is why, here is what happened. Most portfolios are a gallery. The ones that got attention were the ones where I could say why that screen and not another one.

**Being able to describe a failure precisely.** The most useful thing I own professionally is a set of specific, unflattering stories. Four months building something [a landing page would have answered in nine days](/writing/mvp-vs-prototype/). Three weeks on [a design system a four-person team never used](/writing/good-product-design/). Those come up in almost every serious conversation, and they do more work than any credential, because they are evidence of judgement rather than a claim of it.

**Talking to people already doing it.** Unglamorous and effective. Most of the work I have had came from somebody I had once spent an hour talking to about something unrelated.

None of that requires a certificate. All of it requires having done something, and having paid attention while doing it.

## Where a certificate genuinely does help

This is the part the "just build a portfolio" crowd skips, and it is where I think they are wrong.

**When an automated filter stands between you and a human.** Larger companies and agencies do screen on keywords, and a recognisable credential can be the difference between a CV being read and not. That is not a comment on the value of the knowledge. It is a comment on the pipeline, and pretending the pipeline does not exist helps nobody who is stuck in it.

**When you are changing career and have no structure.** If you are coming from teaching, or law, or hospitality, with no projects, no colleagues in the field and no deadlines, then what a good course sells is not knowledge. It is **structure, feedback, and a reason to finish things.** Those are genuinely hard to manufacture alone. The honest test is whether you would have built the same portfolio without the course. For some people the answer is obviously yes and the money is wasted. For plenty of others it is no, and that is worth paying for.

I would not have said this five years ago, and five years ago I was wrong. My route worked for me and I mistook that for it being *the* route.

## What I would do with the money instead, if you have the choice

Find a real problem with a real person attached. A local business with a booking flow nobody completes. A charity with a form that loses people. A friend’s side project. Something where somebody notices whether it works.

Then do the whole thing, including the uncomfortable part: watch a person use it, in silence, without helping. That hour teaches you more about [whether feedback is real or just polite](/tools/is-user-feedback-real-or-just-polite/) than any module on research methods, because you will watch somebody struggle and then tell you it was fine.

One project like that, described honestly, is worth more than any certificate — for the specific reason that it produces a story about a decision that had consequences, and a course cannot manufacture consequences.

## The honest summary

A certificate is a signal, not a skill. If you need the signal, buy it and use it without apology. If you already have the thing it signals, you are buying a receipt.

What nobody can sell you is the part that actually decides this job: having been wrong about something in a way that cost you, and being able to say what you took from the shape of it. That comes from doing the work, getting it wrong, and paying attention. It is free, it is slow, and it is the only route I know that reliably works.
