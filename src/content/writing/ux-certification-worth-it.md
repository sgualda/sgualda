Short answer: I have one. The Google UX Design certificate, and a master’s degree with a title on it. In all the years since, not one client, recruiter or hiring manager has asked to see either.

That is a stranger answer than "do not bother", and I think it is a more useful one, because it comes from somebody who paid.

## What the certificate actually did for me

I did it while I was moving into UX from building things with code and doing visual work. What I got from it was not really knowledge — most of the content is available free, and anybody who tells you otherwise is selling something. What I got was **structure**: a running order, deadlines, shared vocabulary, and a reason to finish things instead of leaving them at 70%. The master’s did the same job on a bigger scale.

I do not regret either. At that moment I needed scaffolding and scaffolding is worth paying for.

But I have to be honest about what happened next: **the market never once checked**. The portfolio checked, every single time.

## What actually got me work

Three things, in the order they mattered.

**Projects with a decision in them.** Not screens. A decision: we could have done A or B, we did B, here is why, here is what happened. Most portfolios are a gallery. The ones that got attention were the ones where I could say why that screen and not another one.

**Being able to describe a failure precisely.** The most useful thing I own professionally is a set of specific, unflattering stories. Four months building something [a landing page would have answered in nine days](/writing/mvp-vs-prototype/). Three weeks on [a design system a four-person team never used](/writing/good-product-design/). Those come up in almost every serious conversation, and they do more work than any credential, because they are evidence of judgement rather than a claim of it.

**Talking to people already doing it.** Unglamorous and effective. Most of the work I have had came from somebody I had once spent an hour talking to about something unrelated.

None of that came from the course. All of it came from having done something, and having paid attention while doing it.

## So why do I still say it is not necessary

Because a certificate is a **way of learning, not a way of proving** — and almost everybody sells it as the second thing.

If you buy it as a way of learning, you are buying structure, and structure is a legitimate purchase. If you buy it as proof, you are buying a receipt for a claim nobody will ask you to back up, because they will ask about the work instead.

I used to think the credential was the entry ticket. It was not. It was scaffolding, and scaffolding is only worth the price if the alternative is that you never build anything.

## Where a certificate genuinely does help

This is the part the "just build a portfolio" crowd skips, and it is where I think they are wrong — "build a portfolio instead" is very easy advice to give once you already have a portfolio.

**When an automated filter stands between you and a human.** Larger companies and agencies do screen on keywords, and a recognisable credential can be the difference between a CV being read and not. That is not a comment on the value of the knowledge. It is a comment on the pipeline, and pretending the pipeline does not exist helps nobody who is stuck in it.

**When you are changing career and have no structure.** If you are coming from teaching, or law, or hospitality, with no projects, no colleagues in the field and no deadlines, then what a good course sells is not knowledge. It is **structure, feedback, and a reason to finish things.** I know, because that is exactly what I bought. The honest test is whether you would have built the same portfolio without the course. For some people the answer is obviously yes and the money is wasted. For plenty of others it is no, and that is worth paying for.

## What I would do with the money instead, if you have the choice

Find a real problem with a real person attached. A local business with a booking flow nobody completes. A charity with a form that loses people. A friend’s side project. Something where somebody notices whether it works.

Then do the whole thing, including the uncomfortable part: watch a person use it, in silence, without helping. That hour teaches you more about [whether feedback is real or just polite](/tools/is-user-feedback-real-or-just-polite/) than any module on research methods, because you will watch somebody struggle and then tell you it was fine.

One project like that, described honestly, is worth more than any certificate — for the specific reason that it produces a story about a decision that had consequences, and a course cannot manufacture consequences.

## The honest summary

A certificate is a signal, not a skill. I hold two of them and neither has ever been read. What has been read, every single time, is the work.

So: if you need the structure, buy it and use it without apology. If what you are actually buying is the signal, understand that you are buying a receipt.

What nobody can sell you is the part that decides this job: having been wrong about something in a way that cost you, and being able to say what you took from the shape of it. That comes from doing the work, getting it wrong, and paying attention. It is free, it is slow, and it is the only route I know that reliably works.
